## The same question under three sampling rules

Question: what is the regional mean stunting prevalence, latest reported year per country, unweighted?

| Sample | Rule | Countries | n | Mean, per cent | Difference from full frame |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A, full frame | every country with any report | all | 8 | 30.2 | reference |
| B, recent reporters | latest report within three years of 2024 | Burundi, Ethiopia, Kenya, Rwanda, Somalia, South Sudan, Uganda, United Republic of Tanzania | 8 | 30.2 | +0.0 points |
| C, better performers | latest value at or below the regional median of 27.5 | Kenya, Somalia, Uganda, United Republic of Tanzania | 4 | 23.2 | -7.0 points |

## What the numbers show

Sample B moves the regional mean by +0.0 percentage points and sample C by -7.0 points, without a single underlying observation changing. The frame is identical in all three rows. Only the rule that selected countries differs.

Sample B moves the mean by zero, and that zero is the more important result of the two. Indicator 2.2.1 is a modelled estimate: the Joint Child Malnutrition Estimates publish a value for every country-year, so the frame contains no missing cells to select on. A database with no gaps is not a database with no missing data; the gaps were filled by a model before you arrived. Selection on reporting recency therefore shows nothing here, and would show a great deal on a survey-reported indicator such as SDG 5.b.1 or SDG 4.1.1, where a country appears only in the years it fielded a survey. Week 2 repeats this exercise on a survey-reported indicator so the recency mechanism can be seen biting.

Sample C is the bias that operates in advocacy: selecting on the outcome itself. A ministry that quotes only the better-performing half of the region is not lying about any number. It is lying about the set the numbers were drawn from.

## Limitations

- The mean is unweighted by population, so a small country counts the same as a large one. A population-weighted mean would answer a different question and could move in the opposite direction.
- Latest-year values come from different calendar years across countries, so sample A mixes years. That mixture is itself a form of the bias under study.
- Because 2.2.1 is modelled, the coverage table shows complete reporting for all eight countries. Completeness here is a property of the estimation method, not of national statistical systems, and must not be read as evidence that all eight countries fielded surveys in all twenty-five years.
- SDG 2.2.1 values are modelled estimates from the Joint Child Malnutrition Estimates, not direct survey counts, so part of the between-country variation is model behaviour rather than observation.
